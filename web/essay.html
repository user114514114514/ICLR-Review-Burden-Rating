When Submission Volume Becomes Review Burden: An Open Attempt to Quantify Conference Review Load

In recent years, top machine learning conferences have experienced unprecedented surges in submission volume. Taking ICLR as a prime example, the 2026 cycle received nearly 20,000 valid submissions, generating more than 76,000 official peer reviews; for the subsequent 2027 cycle, paper registrations on OpenReview even exceeded 62,000.

Peer review is not an automated industrial pipeline; it relies entirely on the finite, non-renewable time and cognitive effort of fellow researchers. Across online discussions in the research community, one observation has resonated widely: the factor truly straining the peer review system is not merely that more people are doing research, but that within a single deadline, certain accounts or groups batch-submit multiple unpolished, unvetted low-quality manuscripts. This "lottery" approach—banking on reviewer variance to push lucky papers over the acceptance line—privatizes the speculative upside for a handful of individuals while imposing a massive, uncompensated review burden on the entire academic community.

In recent years, with the rapid rise and widespread adoption of generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs), the barrier to mass-producing papers has collapsed, aggravating this trend even further. We want to state our position unambiguously: we do not oppose using AI to assist research. On the contrary, we believe the efficiency gains and lowered barriers enabled by AI are genuinely positive developments for science. They empower researchers—especially junior scholars and interdisciplinary teams previously constrained by coding bandwidth, computing budgets, or non-native academic English—to test and realize creative ideas at far lower friction and cost. What we oppose is bypassing substantive human thinking, using LLMs to batch-generate and submit unvetted, low-quality papers that offer no meaningful scientific contribution. This is also one of the central reasons our analysis focuses strictly on 2024 and beyond: it was during this period that automated, LLM-facilitated bulk submission escalated into an undeniable systemic issue.

In this context, why did we choose ICLR data? Because ICLR (powered by OpenReview) preserves and publicly exposes its entire historical submission record. Unlike the vast majority of traditional academic conferences and journals that publish only accepted papers while locking rejected and withdrawn manuscripts in a proprietary black box, ICLR maintains a fully auditable public ledger: all submissions—accepted, rejected, and withdrawn—along with official peer reviews and discussion threads, remain accessible to the community. This radical transparency makes submission behaviors truly traceable, making our empirical and objective quantification of review burden possible. Here, we also sincerely call upon more premier academic conferences and leading journals to adopt and promote this fully transparent model. Only when sunlight illuminates the entire peer review cycle can the health of our shared research commons be safeguarded over the long term.

Recognizing this shared challenge across the community, we undertook an open attempt: building a transparent, open-source, auditable metric—the ICLR Review-Burden Score—based on public OpenReview records to objectively characterize this specific pattern of review burden. We are not conference organizers, and this work does not seek to pass moral judgment on any researcher. It is an open data initiative aimed at providing the community with an objective lens to better understand and discuss the peer review ecosystem.

Scope and Guardrails: What We Measure (and What We Deliberately Avoid)

From the outset, our primary concern was preventing metric misuse. We established strict, deliberate boundaries:

  1. This is not an assessment of scientific merit, researcher ethics, or intent. The algorithm reads only public metadata: ratings, confidence scores, final decisions, and author positions. It cannot evaluate subtle theoretical novelty, understand niche subfield controversies, or know if an exploratory submission simply encountered hurried reviewers. A high score indicates only that a public submission record exhibits a statistically dense concentration of low scores; it does not infer deliberate or malicious intent.
  2. Author position is a coarse proxy for responsibility. In modern research, collaboration structures and authorship conventions vary dramatically. While our model sharply down-weights middle and senior author positions, no mathematical formula can perfectly reconstruct who actually ran the experiments or drafted the manuscript.
  3. Scores in the low range have zero comparative meaning. Whether a score is 0, 2, or 4, minor numerical differences in this band are pure statistical noise and must never be interpreted as "one researcher doing worse work than another." Empirical calculations show that under the Peak Score metric, 99% of all authors score at or below roughly 5. For scores below 5, variations are heavily influenced by natural research exploration, collaborative volume, and reviewer subjectivity, possessing virtually zero evaluative value. This metric is never intended to rank or categorize ordinary researchers; its focus rests strictly on anomalous, densely clustered, and visibly low-quality submissions that impose undeniable community strain within a single cycle.
  4. It is an index, not a verdict. The score acts merely as an audit pointer: "This public record exhibits an unusual pattern that warrants closer, human inspection." The only objective evidence remains the underlying manuscripts, reviews, and author rebuttals.

Why Counting Submissions or Rejection Rates Fails

In discussions of conference review load, common proposals include "capping submissions per author" or "penalizing high rejection rates." An examination of the data reveals critical flaws in both approaches:

  • Simply counting submissions penalizes productive, high-quality research. Consider a researcher who coauthors 15 submissions in a single year: 10 are accepted, 2 are borderline rejections that sparked valuable debate, and 3 missed the mark. While these 15 submissions consumed review bandwidth, they represent serious, competitive contributions. Labeling them as a "problematic burden" would be fundamentally unfair.
  • Using rejection rates punishes honest scientific exploration. In competitive venues where acceptance rates hover around 25%, failing to get in is the statistical norm. An author who submits two papers and sees both rejected with respectable scores of 5.5 has participated in good faith. That is normal scientific dialogue, not wasted reviewer effort.

Our core design principle is therefore:

Burden is not about rejection. It is about how far a paper falls below a reasonable quality floor for that year, and whether that rock-bottom quality is repeatedly submitted within the same cycle.

Formulation: From Individual Reviews to Annual Totals

To maintain interpretable physical meaning and guard against rogue outliers, we designed the score in modular steps:

1. Robust Paper Score (Rp​)

For a paper evaluated by reviewers with ratings ri​∈[1,10] and confidence scores ci​∈[1,5]:

First, reviewer confidence should inform the consensus, but no overconfident reviewer should single-handedly dictate the outcome. We apply a gentle linear scale:

qi​=0.6+0.1ci​.

Confidence 1 receives weight 0.7, while confidence 5 receives 1.1 (modulating influence by roughly ±25%).

Second, to protect against rogue or malicious scores (e.g., an unjustified 1 or 10), we compute the median rating mp​=median(r1​,…,rn​) and clip all ratings to within ±2 points of that median:

r~i​=mp​+clip(ri​−mp​,−2,2).

The Robust Review Score Rp​ is then the confidence-weighted mean:

Rp​=∑i​qi​∑i​qi​r~i​​.

2. Adaptive Benchmarks: Reasonable Submission Floor (Ty​) and High Bar (Hy​)

Grading scales shift across conference editions; an absolute cutoff (such as 4.0 or 5.0) would be arbitrary. Instead, we calibrate each year against that edition's accepted papers.

Let Q25,yacc​ and Q75,yacc​ denote the 25th and 75th percentiles of accepted-paper scores for year y. We estimate the dispersion scale parameter as:

sy​=max(1.349Q75,yacc​−Q25,yacc​​,0.5).

From this, we construct the Reasonable Submission Floor:

Ty​=Q25,yacc​−sy​.

This floor is generous: it sits a full standard deviation below the lower quartile of papers that actually got in. Any rejected or withdrawn paper scoring above Ty​ is treated as an ordinary, honorable rejection—incurring zero deficit (pp​=0).

Only papers that fall below Ty​ accumulate a quality deficit:

pp​=[sy​Ty​−Rp​​]+​.

Accepted papers are never penalized, regardless of numerical score (pp​=0).

At the top end, we set a high bar at the third quartile of accepted work, Hy​=Q75,yacc​. Accepted papers exceeding this threshold receive bounded credit:

gp​=3tanh[21​(sy​Rp​−Hy​​)+​].

The hyperbolic tangent strictly caps credit at 3.0, preventing multiple collaborative acceptances from serving as an unlimited license to flood the venue with unvetted drafts.

3. Authorship Weights, Saturating Single-Paper Severity, and Compounding Burden (Ba,y​)

Penalty weights decay with author position k:

αk​=⎩⎨⎧​1.0,0.3,0.1,​k=1,2≤k≤4,k≥5.​

Credit weights βk​ follow a similar decay (1.0,0.2,0.05).

When evaluating how severely an individual sub-floor paper burdens reviewers, extreme deficits must count, but a single paper's deficit should not scale without limit. We introduce a bounded, saturating single-paper severity function:

h(p)=1+2pp​.

This function satisfies h(0)=0, h(0.5)=0.25, h(1)≈0.333, h(2)=0.4, and asymptotically approaches 0.5 as p→∞. The author rank coefficient αk​ operates strictly outside the severity transform, giving each paper a factor of 1+αk​h(p). Consequently, the maximum multiplicative factor per paper is naturally bounded: at most 1.5 for a first author (α=1.0), 1.15 for ranks 2–4 (α=0.3), and 1.05 for rank 5+ (α=0.1).

For author a in year y, all reviewed low-quality papers with positive deficit (pp​>0) combine into the Compounded Burden Term:

Ba,y​=4​p∈La,y​∏​(1+αk(a,p)​1+2pp​pp​​)−1​.

Why a product with saturation? An occasional weak paper is an ordinary risk of research; a linear sum would excessively penalize occasional stumbles. Under a product formulation, a single weak paper incurs a small penalty. Crucially, the saturating severity function ensures that severe single-paper deficits still count, but their marginal effect saturates: repeated low-quality submissions within the same cycle decisively dominate a small number of isolated, rock-bottom papers.

Positive credit Ga,y​ provides a capped linear offset:

Ga,y​=min(3.0,p∑​βk(a,p)​gp​).

4. Desk Rejects and Portfolio Deficit Ratio (ρy​)

Desk rejects (formatting, anonymity, or page-limit violations) typically do not exhaust multiple full review cycles, but repeated desk rejects in the same cycle demonstrate negligence. We assign them a small, separate linear term:

Da,y​=0.1d∈DRa,y​∑​αk(a,d)​.

We also compute the ratio of problematic submissions to total decided papers (Nbad​=Nlow​+NDR​):

ρy​=Nbad​+Nacc​Nbad​​.

This cleanly separates someone with 3 problematic papers out of 4 submissions (ρ=0.75) from a productive group with 3 problematic papers alongside 10 accepted papers (ρ=0.23).

5. Independent Annual Scores and Peak Score Ranking

Combining all modules, the author's Annual Score for year y is:

Aa,y​=(1+ρy​)max(0,Ba,y​−Ga,y​)+Nbad,a,y​Da,y​​

We adhere to an essential design rule: each year stands completely on its own, with no cross-year accumulation.

Accumulating scores over time effectively penalizes academic longevity: an author active in the community for a decade would automatically carry a higher score than a newcomer, even if their recent submissions were entirely responsible. Science is iterative; each conference edition should start with a clean slate.

When a cross-year ranking is needed, the system reports the author's Peak Score:

Peak Scorea​=ymax​Aa,y​.

The Peak Score asks a clear, bounded question: What was this author's single most burdensome cycle on record, and how intense was it? It isolates peak bulk-dumping episodes without penalizing the length of a career.

Data Discipline: Strict Identity Binding

This project operates on the public qhjqhj00/iclr-openreview-reviews dataset, with scoring strictly confined to ICLR 2024 through 2026 (38,890 submissions), where data schemas are most consistent and submission surges have been most acute.

On author identity, we enforce strict conservatism: an author position is scored if and only if it links cleanly to a canonical OpenReview Profile ID (~Given_Family1). We do not perform fuzzy name matching, email domain clustering, or graph-based inferences. Unresolved author positions remain unresolved; the underlying papers still contribute to global conference calibrations, but are never attributed to individual author profiles. While this leaves roughly 4.7% of author positions unresolved in the current snapshot, we believe this is the right ethical boundary: mistakenly associating a low-quality paper with an innocent researcher is far worse than temporarily omitting an unresolved one.

Conclusion

Across all 93,515 authors analyzed in ICLR 2024–2026, the empirical distribution reveals a clear reality: under the Peak Score metric, 99% of authors score at or below roughly 5 (median around 0.35, 75% below 1.02, and 90% below 2.93). We reiterate emphatically: scores below 5 are subject to natural exploratory variance, collaboration footprints, and reviewer subjectivity, carrying virtually zero evaluative value. The vast majority of researchers submit thoughtfully, and normal exploratory misses, early-career learning curves, and occasional reviewer bad luck fall comfortably within this range—which the peer review commons can and should readily accommodate. The strain on peer review is driven solely by an extreme tail of around 1%. Our focus rests strictly on anomalous, densely clustered, and visibly low-quality submissions within a single cycle.

An open-source metric cannot fix complex academic publishing incentives on its own. However, public, transparent data is the prerequisite for grounded discussion. We hope this attempt provides the community with an objective, auditable window into public review records, helping support more constructive conversations on preserving our shared peer review commons.

当投稿量变成审稿负担：对学术会议审稿压力的一次量化尝试

近年来，顶级机器学习会议的投稿量呈现出前所未有的井喷趋势。以 ICLR 为例，2026 年的有效投稿量接近两万篇，由此产生的官方同行评审超过七万六千份。在紧随其后的 ICLR 2027 周期中，OpenReview 上的投稿取号甚至突破了 62,000 篇。

同行评审不是自动化流水线，它完全依赖于同行学者有限且不可再生的时间与精力。在学术社区的网络讨论中，一个现象引起了广泛共鸣：真正压垮审稿体系的，并不单纯是“整体做科研的人数增加”，而是在同一个截稿周期内，部分账号或团队批量提交多篇缺乏基本打磨与验证的低质量论文。这种把顶会当作“买彩票”的行为，寄希望于评审人的随机方差来博取过审概率，实质上是将极少数个体的投机收益私有化，而将巨大的评审成本强加给整个学术社区。

特别是近年来，伴随着生成式 AI（Generative AI）与大语言模型（LLM）的普及，论文批量生产的门槛被大幅拉低，使得这种现象愈演愈烈。我们必须明确阐述我们的立场：我们绝不反对使用 AI 辅助科研。 相反，我们认为 AI 带来的效率提升与门槛下降对学界是一件极具建设性的好事——它让许多此前受限于算力、编程经验或学术英语表达的新鲜想法，能够以更低的试错成本被快速验证与实现，极大地赋能了广大科研工作者。我们所反对的，是不经过自己深入思考，直接利用大语言模型批量自动拼凑并提交大量粗制滥造、对科研毫无实质贡献的低质量论文。这也是我们将研究数据严格聚焦在 2024 年及以后的核心原因之一：正是从这一时期开始，LLM 工具的全面普及使得自动化批量灌稿的问题变得极其显著。

在这样的背景下，为什么我们选择以 ICLR 作为切入点？因为 ICLR（依托 OpenReview 平台）保留并公开了完整的历史投稿记录。与绝大多数仅公布录用论文、将所有未过审和撤稿稿件锁在黑盒里的传统学术会议与期刊不同，ICLR 完整公开了包括接收、被拒、撤稿在内的全部投稿，以及透明的官方评审意见和讨论记录。这种彻底的公开透明，让所有投稿行为真正做到了“有迹可循”，才使得我们今天对审稿负担的客观量化与理性审计成为可能。在此，我们也真诚呼吁更多顶级学术会议与知名学术期刊，能够学习并推行这种全面公开透明的运作模式，唯有让阳光照进整个同行评审周期，学术公地才能获得长久健康的保障。

看到社区中广泛存在的困扰，我们做了一次尝试：基于 OpenReview 的公开历史数据，构建一套透明、开源、可审计的量化指标——ICLR Review-Burden Score（审稿负担评分），用来客观刻画这种特定模式的审稿负担。我们不是会议官方，这项工作也无意对任何学者进行道德定性。它只是一次公开的数据尝试，希望能为学术社区提供一个理解和讨论审稿生态的客观视角。

指标的定位：我们在度量什么，坚决回避什么

在设计指标之初，我们最警惕的就是指标被误用或滥用。我们给自己设定了明确的边界：

  1. 这不是对学者学术能力、人品或动机的评判。 算法只读取公开的元数据（分数、置信度、决议与作者署名次序）。它无法评估理论证明的微妙创新，不懂小众领域的学术争议，也无法获知某篇探索性工作是否恰好遇到了草率的审稿人。得分高只代表该公开记录在统计上呈现出某种密集低分的特征，绝不代表作者主观恶意灌稿。
  2. 作者署名次序是对实际贡献的粗糙近似。 在现代科研合作中，团队结构与挂名惯例极其多样。尽管我们在模型中大幅调低了后序作者的权重，但没有任何数学规则能准确还原谁实际负责了论文的撰写与实验。
  3. 低分区间没有任何优劣比较的意义。 无论是 0 分、2 分还是 4 分，处于这一区间的分数细微差异纯属统计噪声，绝不能作为“谁的研究更差”的评判依据。全库实际测算表明，在峰值分（Peak Score）口径下，99% 的学者得分基本小于等于 5。对于分数小于 5 的绝大多数情况，数值波动深受科研经验积累、合作规模以及审稿人主观方差等多种客观现实因素的影响，基本没有评判价值。 我们绝不将该指标用于给普通学者排座次，算法只重点关注异常的密集显著低质量投稿——即在同一年里，由异常密集且断崖式偏离及格线的稿件所造成的无法忽视的审稿负担。
  4. 它是一张索引，而不是一份判词。 分数的作用仅仅是提示：“这份公开记录中存在一种值得带着审慎态度去查验的模式。”真正客观的证据始终是底层的论文、审稿意见与作者的反驳记录。

为什么直接数投稿量或计算拒稿率是错误的？

在针对审稿负担的讨论中，常见的建议是“限制每人投稿上限”或“按拒稿率施加惩罚”。但深入数据后可以发现，这两种直觉都存在明显缺陷：

  • 单纯数投稿量会惩罚健康的学术高产。 设想一位学者在某一年署名了 15 篇投稿，其中 10 篇被录用，2 篇在分数临界线上引发了充分的同行讨论，3 篇落选。这 15 篇投稿固然占用了审稿资源，但它们是高质量、严肃的学术贡献，绝不属于应当被负面标记的负担。
  • 计算拒稿率会误伤正常的学术探索。 在录用率通常仅约 25% 的顶级会议中，绝大多数论文落选是普遍规律。如果一位研究者投了两篇论文，双双以 5.5 分擦肩未进，这是完全健康的科学试错，不该受到任何惩罚。

因此，我们的核心设计原则是：

负担的关键不在于是否落选，而在于论文离当年合理的及格线下潜了多远，以及这种断崖式的低质是否在同一周期内被反复复制。

评分机制：从单篇评审到年度核算

为了让指标具备清晰的物理意义并抵御异常值的干扰，我们采用了如下的分步设计：

1. 单篇鲁棒评分（Rp​）

对于一篇包含若干审稿打分 ri​∈[1,10] 和置信度 ci​∈[1,5] 的论文：

首先，置信度应当被参考，但不能让极度自信的审稿人一票独大。我们对置信度采用平缓的线性调节：

qi​=0.6+0.1ci​.

置信度 1 的权重为 0.7，置信度 5 的权重为 1.1（影响幅度约 ±25%）。

其次，为防范个别审稿人的极端情绪化打分，我们取该论文所有评审分的中位数 mp​=median(r1​,…,rn​)，并将每个原始评分截断在中位数上下 2 分的窗口内：

r~i​=mp​+clip(ri​−mp​,−2,2).

论文的鲁棒得分 Rp​ 即为加权平均分：

Rp​=∑i​qi​∑i​qi​r~i​​.

2. 动态自适应：合理投稿下限（Ty​）与优质标杆（Hy​）

各届会议的审稿松紧尺度存在天然飘移，不能采用静态绝对分。我们以该年份真正被录用的论文（Accepted Papers）为参考基准。

令 Q25,yacc​ 和 Q75,yacc​ 分别为当年已录用论文得分的第 25 和第 75 百分位数，定义尺度参数：

sy​=max(1.349Q75,yacc​−Q25,yacc​​,0.5).

由此设定合理投稿下限（Reasonable Submission Floor）：

Ty​=Q25,yacc​−sy​.

这一底线极其宽容，退到了录用线下四分位整整一个标准差之外。凡是得分在 Ty​ 之上的未录用或撤稿论文，均被视作正常的学术失败，亏空计为零（pp​=0）。

只有当论文评分跌破 Ty​ 时，才产生质量亏空：

pp​=[sy​Ty​−Rp​​]+​.

录用论文不论分值，惩罚永远豁免（pp​=0）。

同时，我们将录用论文的上四分位设为优质标杆 Hy​=Q75,yacc​，超出此线的优质录用论文将给予有上限的信用对冲：

gp​=3tanh[21​(sy​Rp​−Hy​​)+​].

信用上限通过 tanh 严格封顶在 3.0，避免多篇合作录用论文沦为无限抵消劣质投稿的“豁免券”。

3. 作者位次权重、饱和单篇严重度与复利累乘（Ba,y​）

惩罚权重随作者署名位次 k 衰减：

αk​=⎩⎨⎧​1.0,0.3,0.1,​k=1,2≤k≤4,k≥5.​

优质论文的奖励权重 βk​ 也遵循类似梯度（1.0,0.2,0.05）。

在度量单篇低分论文的严重程度时，极低的分数理应产生约束，但单篇亏空不应无限线性放大。我们引入有界的单篇饱和严重度函数：

h(p)=1+2pp​.

该函数满足 h(0)=0，h(0.5)=0.25，h(1)≈0.333，h(2)=0.4，当亏空 p→∞ 时，h(p) 逐渐饱和收敛至 0.5。作者位次权重 αk​ 严格位于严重度函数外部，单篇乘积因子表述为 1+αk​h(p)。因此单篇论文带来的乘积因子具有天然上限（第一作者最大 1.5，第 2–4 作者最大 1.15，第 5 作者及以后最大 1.05）。

对于作者 a 在年份 y，所有存在正亏空（pp​>0）的已评审低质论文通过连乘构成复合累积负担项：

Ba,y​=4​p∈La,y​∏​(1+αk(a,p)​1+2pp​pp​​)−1​.

为什么采用乘积与饱和机制？ 偶发的一篇失误是正常的科研波动，线性相加会过分惩罚偶发失手；而在连乘结构下，单篇低分论文的惩罚极其微弱。引入饱和函数后，单篇严重低分仍然有明确体现，但其边际影响会随着亏空加大而趋于饱和；机制的核心意图在于让在同一周期内多次反复提交低质量论文的行为，显著主导极少数单篇极端超低分的情况。

优质信用 Ga,y​ 提供封顶线性对冲：

Ga,y​=min(3.0,p∑​βk(a,p)​gp​).

4. 格式拒稿（Desk Reject）与结构比率（ρy​）

格式违规或超页导致的 Desk Reject 通常不会完整消耗多轮审稿，但同一年内反复出现同样体现了不合规的投机性。我们赋予其独立的轻量线性项：

Da,y​=0.1d∈DRa,y​∑​αk(a,d)​.

结合该作者当年问题论文数 Nbad​=Nlow​+NDR​ 与录用论文数 Nacc​，定义结构比率：

ρy​=Nbad​+Nacc​Nbad​​.

以此区分“投 4 篇有 3 篇严重不合格”与“在 10 篇录用中夹杂了 3 篇未过审”这两种本质不同的场景。

5. 单年独立核算与跨年峰值分（Peak Score）

综合各模块，作者在年份 y 的年度审稿负担评分（Annual Score）为：

Aa,y​=(1+ρy​)max(0,Ba,y​−Ga,y​)+Nbad,a,y​Da,y​​

我们在此坚持一个核心原则：每年独立核算，不搞跨年累加。

跨年累加在客观上会变成对“学术资历”的隐性惩罚——一个在领域内活跃了十年的资深学者，即使近年来投稿十分节制，累计得分也天然高于新人。科学研究是动态迭代的，每一届会议都应是一张全新的白板。

当需要跨年份进行全局排名时，系统展示的是作者在所有年份中的最高单年分——峰值分（Peak Score）：

Peak Scorea​=ymax​Aa,y​.

峰值分聚焦于一个清晰明确的问题：该作者在公开记录中，造成最大单年审稿压力的周期的强度是多少？ 这样既能如实反映极端批量投稿的冲击力，又避免将学术生涯长度变为负累。

数据原则：严格保守的身份绑定

本项目基于公开的 qhjqhj00/iclr-openreview-reviews 数据集，计分范围严格限制在数据结构规范、激增现象明显的 ICLR 2024 至 2026 年（共 38,890 篇投稿）。

在作者身份匹配上，我们坚持极度保守的标准：仅当投稿明确绑定了规范的 OpenReview Profile ID（如 ~Given_Family1）时，才计入个人得分。我们坚决不做基于姓名的模糊猜测，不做邮箱后缀聚类，也不做关系图谱推断。无法确切解析的作者槽位一律标记为“未解析”，论文本身仍参与全会基准线计算，但绝不挂到任何具体学者的名下。虽然这导致当前快照中有约 4.7% 的作者槽位未被解析，但我们认为这是必须坚守的底线：在学术共同体中，错判一个无辜同名的同行，其伤害远甚于漏掉一个真实作者。

结语

在对 2024–2026 年间全部 93,515 名作者进行实际测算后，数据呈现出的基本事实是：在峰值分（Peak Score）下，99% 的作者得分基本小于等于 5（中位数约为 0.35，75% 的作者得分在 1.02 以下，90% 在 2.93 以下）。我们必须再次强调：分数小于 5 的情况深受学术探索、合作规模与审稿随机性等多种正常因素的影响，基本没有评判价值。 绝大多数学者都在严谨治学，正常的科研试错和偶发的评审波动完全处于这一区间之内，这是审稿公地完全能够且应当包容的健康学术常态。审稿体系所面临的真正挑战，仅仅来自于分布末端不到 1% 的极端尾部。我们只重点关注在单届会议中异常的密集显著低质量投稿。

一个开源工具不可能改变复杂的学术评价生态，但公开透明的数据是一切理性探讨的起点。我们希望这项尝试能够为学术社区提供一个客观、中立、基于公开证据的观察窗口，帮助大家共同探讨如何维护脆弱的同行评审公地。
